If you still have a company, property, or cash in South Africa

In short. Forms to consider if you still have a company, property, a directorship, or cash.

This is not tax advice. This page is general information for information purposes only. It is not legal advice and it is not tax advice. Confirm it with a qualified tax practitioner. If you have tax in both South Africa and the United States, speak to a cross-border tax practitioner. The sources on this page were read on 27 October 2026.

What to consider, not what to do

The ordinary case, an employee who has come to find a job, is Tax when you start work. This page is for three further questions people ask:

  • I own properties through companies in South Africa.
  • I am still a director of a South African company.
  • Should I move my cash to the United States, or leave it in South Africa?

The pages below name the official forms and the official rules. They do not tell you to sell, to resign, to keep the shares, or to move the money. A cross-border tax practitioner applies them to your facts.

I own properties through companies

United States information returns. If you are a United States person and you own shares in a foreign corporation, the IRS instructions for Form 5471, Information Return of U.S. Persons With Respect to Certain Foreign Corporations (revision of December 2025), set out categories of filers. There are exceptions in the instructions. A category is not a decision that you must file [source]:

  • Category 1 is a United States shareholder of a foreign corporation that was a section 965 specified foreign corporation, on the terms in the instructions.
  • Category 2 includes a United States citizen or resident who is an officer or director of a foreign corporation in which a United States person has acquired stock that meets the 10 percent stock ownership requirement, or an additional 10 percent or more.
  • Category 3 includes a United States person who acquires stock to meet the 10 percent requirement, a person who becomes a United States person while meeting that requirement, and a United States person who disposes of enough stock to fall below it. Becoming a United States tax resident while you already own 10 percent is one of the cases the instructions list.
  • Category 4 is a United States person who had control of a foreign corporation. Control, for this category, is more than 50 percent of the voting power or more than 50 percent of the value.
  • Category 5 is a United States shareholder who owned stock in a controlled foreign corporation on the last day in the year on which it was a controlled foreign corporation. For this category, a United States shareholder owns 10 percent or more of the voting power or value. A controlled foreign corporation is a foreign corporation in which United States shareholders own, on any day of its tax year, more than 50 percent of the voting power or more than 50 percent of the value.

The same instructions say United States shareholders of a controlled foreign corporation that has subpart F income must report that income on their tax returns. Schedule I-1 of Form 5471 is information used for the inclusion under section 951A. The instructions tell the shareholder to use Form 8992 for that calculation, and they call it global intangible low-taxed income (GILTI). The IRS draft forms page lists draft Instructions for Form 8992 with a posted date of 21 October 2026. The file is marked draft and not for filing. Its heading uses the revision label December 2026, and a line in the file prints 19 October 2026. That draft says Public Law 119-21 renamed GILTI to net CFC tested income (NCTI) for taxable years beginning after 31 December 2025, and changed the calculation. The About Form 8992 page, reviewed on 22 July 2026, still describes the form as the GILTI calculation. December 2026 is the revision label printed on the draft. It is not a month this guide treats as already reached. Check which text applies to the year you file. This guide does not restate the formula [source, source, source, source].

A passive foreign investment company. Form 8621 is the information return for a shareholder of a passive foreign investment company. The instructions say a foreign corporation is one if 75 percent or more of its gross income for the year is passive income, or if at least 50 percent of its assets produce passive income or are held to produce it. A United States person files Form 8621 in the five situations the instructions list, including certain distributions, certain dispositions, and an annual report under section 1298(f). There is also an overlap rule for stock that is both a controlled foreign corporation and a passive foreign investment company. This page does not decide that a company which holds South African property meets either test [source].

Form 8938. An interest in a foreign entity, and stock issued by someone other than a United States person, can be specified foreign financial assets. The thresholds are on Reporting SA accounts [source].

South African tax on the shares and the property.

  • Dividends. SARS says dividends tax is 20 percent, from 22 February 2017, on dividends paid by a resident company, and that the rate can be reduced by a double tax agreement. The non-resident beneficial owner submits the declaration forms on the SARS website to the company or the regulated intermediary, or the tax is withheld. The treaty, Article 10, caps the tax at 5 percent of the gross dividend if the beneficial owner is a company that holds directly at least 10 percent of the voting stock, and at 15 percent in all other cases. An individual shareholder is in the 15 percent group. The treaty rate is a cap. SARS still requires the declaration [source, source].
  • Capital gains for a non-resident. SARS says a non-resident is subject to capital gains tax on immovable property in South Africa, and on equity shares when 80 percent or more of the market value of those shares is attributable directly or indirectly to immovable property in South Africa. The SARS tax guide for share owners states both that 80 percent test and a second test: the person, alone or together with a connected person, directly or indirectly holds at least 20 percent of the equity shares. The public "Tax and Non-Residents" page states the 80 percent test and does not repeat the 20 percent holding test. Both pages are SARS pages. The conflict is flagged below [source, source, source].
  • The treaty. Article 13 says gains from the alienation of real property situated in the other country may be taxed in that country. For this treaty, real property situated in the other country includes an equivalent interest in real property in South Africa. Article 6 says income from immovable property situated in the other country may be taxed in that country [source].
  • Withholding when a non-resident sells. Section 35A, as the SARS external guide describes it, makes the buyer withhold tax from the price paid to a non-resident seller of immovable property in South Africa. The guide sets the withholding at 7.5 percent if the seller is a natural person (SARS writes 7,5 percent), 10 percent if the seller is a company, and 15 percent if the seller is a trust. It applies when the amount payable is more than R2 million, and then it applies to the full price. It does not apply when the amount payable is R2 million or less. The amount withheld is an advance payment toward the seller's normal tax for that year, not the final tax. The seller can apply for a directive so that less, or nothing, is withheld. The buyer who is a South African resident pays SARS within the period the guide states for a resident, and a non-resident buyer has a longer period. Read the guide for those day counts and for the penalty if the buyer does not pay [source].
  • Rent. SARS says the source of rental income is generally where the property is used day to day. Rental from South African property, received by a non-resident, is subject to normal tax in South Africa. Expenses such as rates and taxes, bond interest, insurance and repairs may be claimed, subject to conditions SARS states. Article 6 allows South Africa to tax that income as well. A United States resident who is also taxed on it in the United States looks to the foreign tax credit [source, source, source].

I am still a director

Can a non-resident be a director? A sentence that a director must live in South Africa was not found on the Companies and Intellectual Property Commission (CIPC) or gov.za pages checked for this guide. What those pages do say is that a foreign national can be appointed, and that CIPC runs a foreigner-assurance step before the appointment. From 1 December 2023, CIPC's notice says a foreign national submits information and a certified passport or identity document on e-Services before being appointed as a director of a company, close corporation or co-operative. The appointment steps for a passport holder are in CIPC's director-amendment guide. A 2023 amendment of the Companies Regulations, published on gov.za, requires a director who is not South African to be recorded with a passport number, the country of issue, and the country of birth where that differs from the country of issue [source, source, source].

South African tax on directors' fees. The SARS guide for employers in respect of employees' tax for the 2026 tax year says the definition of employee includes a director of a private company, so remuneration paid to that director is subject to employees' tax. The same guide says that where the non-executive director is a non-resident, the directors' fee is regarded as remuneration and the employer must deduct employees' tax. The certificate code guide describes code 3621 as directors' remuneration received from a South African source by a non-resident non-executive director [source, source].

The treaty. Article 16 says directors' fees and other remuneration that a resident of one country derives for services rendered in the other country, in the capacity of a member of the board of a company that is a resident of that other country, may be taxed in that other country. The article is about services rendered in the other country. Whether fees for work done from the United States were "rendered in" South Africa is not decided on these pages [source].

United States tax, and work permission. Permission to work is an immigration question. USCIS says refugees may work from the day they arrive. Tax is a different question. If you are a United States resident alien, Publication 519 taxes you on worldwide income, which includes directors' fees from a South African company. The two rules sit side by side. One does not cancel the other [source, source].

Should I move my cash or leave it in South Africa?

There is no official page that answers that question for you. These are the rules that bear on it.

  • If you are still a South African tax resident, the single discretionary allowance and the R10 million foreign capital allowance are the SARB routes summarised on Moving money and pensions. The rand amount of the single discretionary allowance is in conflict between the April 2026 circular and the older public guides. Do not pick a number from memory [source, source].
  • If you have ceased to be a South African tax resident, SARB's individuals guide sets the transfer rules: tax compliance status, the R10 million calendar-year figure, a stricter path above that, a once-off travel allowance in the year you cease, and a once-off cash balance of not more than R100 000 without reference to SARS. SARS asks, for an international-transfer approval, for proof that you have ceased and for the capital gains calculation on the deemed disposal [source, source].
  • The account itself. SARB defines a non-resident rand account. It does not, on the pages checked, order you to close a South African account when you cease to be a tax resident [source].
  • United States tax on the interest. A resident alien is taxed on worldwide income. Interest from a South African account is part of that income. You report amounts on the United States return in United States dollars. The IRS says you translate foreign-currency items at the exchange rate when you receive, pay or accrue them [source, source].
  • Reports. The same account can trigger FinCEN Form 114 if the aggregate of foreign accounts exceeded $10,000 at any time in the year, and Form 8938 if the specified-asset thresholds are met. See Reporting SA accounts [source, source].
  • A gift or an inheritance from outside the United States. The IRS says a United States person files Form 3520 to report, among other things, gifts or bequests of more than $100,000 from a nonresident alien individual or a foreign estate, and gifts from a foreign corporation or foreign partnership that exceed the threshold the IRS publishes for section 6039F. That second threshold is on the IRS inflation-adjustment page the filing-requirements article points to. This guide does not copy a figure it did not open on that list. The same IRS article says a foreign trust can include a foreign pension or retirement plan that is treated as a trust. Form 8938 instructions say Revenue Procedure 2020-17 can exempt some tax-favoured foreign retirement trusts from Form 3520, and that the exemption does not remove Form 8938 [source, source].
  • A gain that exists only because the rand moved. The IRS foreign-currency page tells you how to translate income and expenses into dollars. It does not, on that page, set out a separate rule for a gain or loss that arises only because cash sat in a rand account while the exchange rate moved. That rule is left out. Ask a practitioner if it matters to the account [source].

Conflicts and gaps

  • GILTI and NCTI: the December 2025 Form 5471 instructions and the About Form 8992 page, reviewed on 22 July 2026, say GILTI. The IRS draft forms page posted draft Instructions for Form 8992 on 21 October 2026. The file prints 19 October 2026, is marked draft and not for filing, and its heading uses the revision label December 2026. That draft says the name is net CFC tested income for taxable years beginning after 31 December 2025. December 2026 is the label on the draft, not a month that has arrived [source, source, source, source].
  • Land-rich shares: the non-residents page states an 80 percent market-value test. The share-owners guide states that test and a 20 percent holding test. This guide does not drop either sentence [source, source].
  • The single discretionary allowance rand amount is in conflict. See Moving money and pensions [source, source, source].
  • A Companies Act rule that a director must live in South Africa: not found on the pages checked. The pages that were found describe how a passport holder is appointed [source, source].
  • Whether directors' fees earned while you sit in the United States are from a South African source: the certificate code is described as South African-source remuneration, and Article 16 is about services rendered in the other country. The pages do not apply those sentences to remote work [source, source].

What these pages leave out

  • Any recommendation to move cash, to keep it, to sell property, or to resign a directorship.
  • The inflation-adjusted FBAR penalty in dollars. The IRS says the statutory maximums are adjusted annually and does not need this guide to invent the current figure [source].
  • A statement that an exit tax on an ordinary employee is usually small. SARS does not say that [source].
  • State income tax, other than the Florida personal-income-tax sentence on the checklist.
